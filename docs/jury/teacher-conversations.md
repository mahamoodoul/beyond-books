# Teacher conversations (evidence for "A real problem")

**Status: 3 of 3 conversations recorded** (Ingrid, Daniel, Fatima; 2026-11-08). Dates and channel still to add. Part A holds the conversations. Part B is the simulated
exercise the team ran beforehand to sharpen the questions; it is kept only as "what we
expected" next to "what we heard" and must not be shown to the jury as evidence.

Three short conversations with real teachers. 10 minutes each is enough: a call, a chat, or a
corridor talk. Write the answers in the teacher's own words where you can. Initials are fine if
the teacher prefers not to be named.

Ask these five questions and note the answers:

1. What do you teach, and which year?
2. When you prepare a lesson that needs visuals (a diagram, a model, a video), what do you do today?
3. How long does that take you per lesson?
4. Where do your students get stuck in this subject?
5. If you could let students click on a 3D model of something you teach, what would it be?

Follow-up that gives you a number for the pitch: "Roughly how many hours a week is that?"

### Conversation 1: Ingrid, 46, naturfag teacher, 17 years' experience

- **Who:** Ingrid, 46, secondary school near Oslo
- **Subject and year:** naturfag (science), Years 8 to 10; currently Year 9, the human body
  (blood circulation, lungs)
- **Date and how (call / chat / in person):** *(add: date and how you spoke)*
- **How they build visual lessons today:** reuses old PowerPoints with small changes; sometimes a
  YouTube video, but "half the videos are either too complicated or too childish"
- **Time per lesson:** about 30 minutes for a topic taught before; 2 to 3 hours for something new.
  Not everything is prepared from scratch each week.
- **Where students get stuck:** understanding how things actually work, not the names. "They can
  memorize the names of heart chambers, but ask them how blood moves through the heart, and
  suddenly they're confused."

- **What this tells us:** the review-and-publish step is a selling point for experienced teachers,
  not a formality; keep the teacher flow short, and lead the demo with the flow of blood through
  the heart, not the part names.

### Conversation 2: Daniel, 29, science and maths teacher, 3 years' experience

- **Who:** Daniel, 29, school in the Oslo area
- **Subject and year:** science and mathematics, Years 6 to 8; mostly Years 7 and 8, currently
  electricity
- **Date and how (call / chat / in person):** *(add: date and how you spoke)*
- **How they build visual lessons today:** "A lot of Googling." Textbook first, then videos or
  something interactive; uses PhET simulations often for physics.
- **Time per lesson:** about an hour for a normal lesson; two to three hours when slides and
  activities are made from scratch
- **Where students get stuck:** engagement varies a lot: "some students understand immediately,
  while others need to see the same thing five different ways." In electricity, they do not
  understand what happens inside a circuit.

- **One sentence we can quote:** "If it's just a rotating 3D model, I don't think that's enough."
- **What this tells us:** for physics teachers the value is cause and effect, not anatomy. Our
  animations and part explanations cover "see what happens"; "change something" (a circuit
  simulator) is not built and is a candidate for the roadmap, not the demo.

### Conversation 3: Fatima, 38, biology and science teacher, 9 years' experience

- **Who:** Fatima, 38, secondary school in the Oslo region
- **Subject and year:** biology and general science, Years 8 to 10; three different classes this
  year
- **Date and how (call / chat / in person):** *(add: date and how you spoke)*
- **How they build visual lessons today:** textbook, PowerPoint, worksheets; videos sometimes, but

- **Time per lesson:** about two hours for a new topic, sometimes more. The bigger cost is

- **Where students get stuck:** the explanation level. One class copes with a complicated

- **What this tells us:** editing generated text before publishing is a must (built). A
  "simplify for this class" option is the clearest feature request from all three conversations;
  the part-insight "simplify" action exists for 3D parts, but not for a whole lesson. Roadmap.

### What we learned

- **The problem in one sentence:** preparing a lesson that needs visuals and a check for
  understanding takes experienced teachers two to three hours per new topic, mostly spent
  hunting for visuals that match the textbook and rewriting explanations for different levels.
- **Numbers we can quote:** new topic 2 to 3 hours (Ingrid), 2 to 3 hours from scratch (Daniel),
  about 2 hours plus adaptation (Fatima); a familiar topic 30 minutes to 1 hour.
- **What all three asked for before trusting it:** the teacher reviews and edits everything
  before students see it (built: nothing is published unreviewed, and lesson text, part
  descriptions and quizzes are editable).
- **What we built because of this:** Daniel's circuit request became the **Labs** tab
  (2026-11-08): students connect a battery, bulbs and a switch with wires and see the bulb light,
  dim or short out, while the teacher watches every student's circuit update live.
- **What they want that we have not built:** a second, simpler version of a lesson without
  redoing it (Fatima). On the roadmap; the pitch should say so rather than overclaim.
- **What this changed in the product and the demo:** lead the demo with the review step and the
  flow of blood through the heart, not the part names; keep the teacher flow to a few clicks
  (Ingrid's "not another complicated system").

### Working problem statement (hypothesis)

Teachers spend significant time combining textbook material, diagrams, videos and quizzes into
understandable lessons. Beyond Books aims to simplify that process by turning textbook chapters
into interactive, teacher-reviewed 3D learning experiences.

### Proposed pilot

Year 9 naturfag, the human circulatory system. Measure actual preparation time, model accuracy,
student comprehension and teacher satisfaction. Target hypothesis: a 50% reduction in preparation
time. This is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated result.
